Freshwater scarcity makes desalination sound like a clean engineering victory: take ocean water in, get drinking water out. Once you actually work on concentrated brines, the problem looks much less heroic and much more like a negotiation with thermodynamics. Salt dissolves into water spontaneously; separating them back out always demands an energy bill.

## The stream left behind

Our work examined how polymer molecular weight affects temperature-swing solvent extraction for concentrated brines. What stayed with me was the stream left behind. Conventional desalination pushes clean water out one end while concentrating a heavier, saltier brine at the other—an environmental burden that still has to go somewhere.

Spending time with that constraint left me with a durable heuristic: engineering rarely erases a difficulty. Usually, it concentrates the difficulty and forces a decision about who, or what, absorbs the residue.

## What gets filtered out

I see a parallel in clinical data. Pulling a tidy analytic cohort out of a messy electronic health record leaves a residue behind: missing measurements, patients excluded by a coding rule, unrecorded confounders. Optimizing the yield is only half the work; the other half is accounting for what got filtered out.
