For more than a century, transportation has become faster. Yet the time people devote to travel has not fallen in proportion. Cesare Marchetti argued that human settlement repeatedly reorganizes around a roughly stable daily travel-time budget: when movement becomes faster, we often travel farther instead of claiming the saved time.

The idea is a broad empirical regularity, not a law that every person obeys. Still, it captures something familiar. A faster road does not necessarily give us a shorter day. It can give us a larger city.

AI may be about to run the same experiment on knowledge work.

## Efficiency Gains Do Not Choose Their Own Destination

Generative AI can reduce the time required for some tasks. In a field study of customer-support agents, access to an AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by about 14 percent on average, with larger gains among less experienced workers. A later field experiment across 66 firms examined how AI changed email, meetings, and after-hours work.

But a productivity gain does not tell us what happens to the saved time.

An individual may finish earlier. A team may raise its quality. A company may increase its expected output. Customers may simply begin to expect faster replies. Often several of these happen at once.

This is why "AI saves time" is an incomplete sentence. The missing question is: *Who gets to allocate the saving?*

Transportation technology often converted speed into distance. Communication technology converted convenience into message volume. AI may convert faster production into a larger surface of work: more drafts, more analyses, more experiments, more follow-ups, and more decisions requiring review.

The tool can be genuinely useful while the day still feels more crowded.

## Cities Accelerate Because Connections Multiply

In *Scale*, Geoffrey West argues that cities do more than concentrate people. They increase the rate at which people encounter one another, exchange information, compete, and cooperate. The same urban intensity associated with innovation and economic activity can also concentrate pollution and disease.

This makes urban life feel faster. It also explains why remote work has not produced a simple end to cities. Digital tools can loosen the physical connection between home and office, but they do not automatically reproduce every form of trust, tacit knowledge, or creative interaction.

Research comparing video and in-person collaboration found that virtual pairs generated fewer creative ideas in the studied tasks, even though they were not worse at selecting among ideas. That does not prove that office work is always superior. It suggests that communication media change different parts of collaboration in different ways.

AI will complicate this distinction further. It may help us prepare for a conversation, translate across languages, preserve organizational memory, and coordinate teams that could never fit in one room. It may also add more interactions than a person can meaningfully process.

## More Connections Are Not the Same as More Relationships

The "Dunbar number" is often simplified into a hard limit of 150 friends. The research is more nuanced. Human social networks appear layered: a small inner circle receives much more time and emotional investment than a much larger group of acquaintances. Online networks can make it easier to keep track of the outer layers without necessarily expanding the inner ones.

Now imagine AI agents maintaining contact, remembering histories, scheduling follow-ups, and drafting personalized messages for thousands of people. Our nominal network could become enormous.

Would our social capacity expand with it?

Some constraints probably would loosen. Memory and coordination are real bottlenecks, and AI can help with both. But trust is built through reciprocal attention. Friendship involves risk, presence, and a willingness to be changed by another person. Delegating the maintenance of a relationship may increase contact while reducing the part that made the relationship meaningful.

The question is therefore not whether AI can raise a number from 150 to 15,000. That precision would be fiction. The better question is which layer of the network AI can enlarge:

- the number of people we can identify;
- the number of conversations we can maintain;
- the number of people we can coordinate with;
- or the number of relationships in which we can genuinely remain present.

Those are different capacities.

## Freedom Requires a Rule for Stopping

Efficiency is usually presented as liberation. Sometimes it is. But systems tend to reinvest slack. A faster commute expands the reachable city. Faster communication expands the inbox. Faster analysis expands the set of questions we can ask before making a decision.

Without a stopping rule, every saved minute becomes capacity for another task.

That rule can be personal: fewer active projects, protected time, or a deliberate limit on responsiveness. It can also be organizational: measure quality rather than message volume, return part of the productivity gain as time, and distinguish communication that needs a person from communication that can be automated.

AI may give us more reach than any previous tool. Whether that reach becomes freedom depends less on the speed of the model than on the social rules around it.

The future of work will not be decided only by how much time AI saves. It will be decided by what we believe saved time is for.
