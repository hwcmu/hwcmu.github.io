Once the treasure has been found, departure looks obvious. The hardships become necessary chapters, the doubts become obstacles overcome, and the person who stayed behind seems to have missed the point. The ending quietly reorganizes everything that came before it.

The final turn of *The Alchemist* makes that temptation vivid. A robber's account of his own dream gives Santiago a clue to treasure back where his journey began. One followed a dream; the other did not. The [plot summary](https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/the-alchemist/summary/) and [teaching guide](https://studylib.net/doc/8336378/a-teacher-s-guide-to---harpercollins-publishers) both identify this contrast. The ending is discussed below.

## Put the Ending Out of Reach

The two characters invite comparison, but they do not form a controlled experiment. Their circumstances, resources, obligations, and subsequent experiences are not held equal. More fundamentally, the story is arranged so that the treasure exists.

That arrangement is part of the pleasure of a fable. It lets a journey carry a meaning that becomes clear on the last page. Difficulty appears within a world whose coherence the author can secure.

A person deciding whether to begin a project does not have that access. They may have an idea, some encouraging signals, limited savings or time, and several possible explanations for why the plan might work. They cannot use the eventual ending as a reason to begin, because it is precisely what they do not yet know.

This does not establish that the robber made a wise choice. We do not know enough to calculate that either. It limits what the contrast can prove about decisions outside the novel.

## A Result Matters Without Settling Every Question

Imagine two people undertaking similar creative projects. One checks the costs, tries a small version, receives encouraging feedback, and still produces work that finds no audience. The other skips those steps and happens to attract attention immediately.

The second person has a better result on that measure. The result is real and worth learning from. Yet we would need more information before recommending their process to everyone else.

I would separate the questions. What happened? What was reasonably knowable when the decision was made? What should this outcome change about the next decision?

The separation works in both directions. Success should prompt investigation rather than automatic imitation. Failure should prompt review rather than automatic exoneration. Calling a decision reasonable under uncertainty must not become a way to refuse every lesson from its consequences.

## What Would Make Another Step Worth Taking?

The preceding essay argues that action can generate information. That is a reason to consider an attempt, not a blanket justification for continuing it.

Before starting, I would write down what I hope to learn, the cost I can absorb, and the observations that would make me reconsider. For a writing project, that could mean finishing a limited piece, seeking specific feedback, and deciding whether to revise the approach before committing to a whole book.

These limits should match the stakes. Some commitments are difficult to reverse, and other people's time or trust cannot be treated as a disposable experiment. A small rehearsal may help without reproducing every feature of the larger decision.

Afterward, I would compare the result with those earlier expectations. What became clearer? What remained unresolved? Is the next step supported by something new, or only by the effort already spent?

## Keep the Courage, Reopen the Judgment

I can value Santiago's willingness to leave without treating his treasure as a guarantee offered to anyone who does the same. The story can make an unexplored possibility feel vivid. It cannot remove the need to judge the terms of our own attempt.

Before knowing the ending, the question is whether the next step is worth taking with what we know and can afford. After knowing it, the task is to learn without pretending we knew all along.
